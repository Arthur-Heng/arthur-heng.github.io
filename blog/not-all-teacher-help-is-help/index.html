The Myth of the Unteachable State: Teachability Is a Teacher Problem

Small agents are usually taught where they are almost right, and left alone where they fail completely. We think that is a misdiagnosis. Those states are not unteachable. The teacher is failing there too.

Heng Wang · November 12, 2026 · ongoing work

There is a piece of folk wisdom in agent distillation: teach the student where it is already close. If the small model was one good action away from solving the task, a nudge from a big model goes a long way. If the small model is hopelessly lost, the nudge is wasted, so don't bother.

It is a reasonable rule, and it has an uncomfortable consequence. The states where the student is hopelessly lost are the states where it most needs a teacher. A method that skips them is tutoring only the questions the student nearly got right.

We went looking for what makes those states so hard to teach. The answer turned out to have little to do with the student.

A big agent takes over from a small one

We distill a large agent into a small one. The student, Qwen3-1.7B, runs the task. We sample a state from its own trajectory. The teacher, Qwen3-32B, takes over from that state and plays on, and the student is fine-tuned on those takeovers. We do this in two environments: ALFWorld, where an agent carries out household tasks in a text world, and WebShop, where it shops for a product that matches a request.

The question is which takeovers are worth learning from.

To answer it we label every state by how the student was doing when the teacher arrived. We let the student try 16 different continuations from that state. If at least one of them solves the task, the state is covered: the student is already close. If all 16 fail, the state is uncovered: left alone, the student has no way out.

The states that need help are the ones being avoided

Much recent work answers the question the same way: stay close to what the student can already do. The mechanisms differ, but the instinct is shared.

  • PACED weights each training problem by how often the student solves it, with a curve that gives almost nothing to problems the student never solves.
  • CSO, built for agents, keeps a teacher's action only if the student can then finish the task from there.
  • Guided-OPD notes that the teacher's supervision is least reliable where the student has drifted far, and responds by keeping the student near the teacher.

In our terms, all of these favor covered states and turn away from uncovered ones, where the student fails completely.

But those are exactly the states where the student most needs help. They are also most of what a weak student produces: in our data, 332 of 389 sampled states in ALFWorld and 333 of 397 in WebShop are uncovered. Avoiding them means leaving the hard five-sixths of the problem untaught.

The closest work to ours goes the other way. OVCSD calls for help only where every student attempt failed, and keeps only the continuations that complete the task. Its teacher, though, is the student itself with extra skill context, and because it only ever intervenes at those states, it cannot say how they compare with the covered ones. That comparison is what we set out to make.

The teacher fails there too

We asked why uncovered states look unteachable, and the answer is not the states. Look at what the teacher does when it takes over.

The teacher succeeds on only 13% and 16% of takeovers at uncovered states, against 82% and 59% at covered ones. The big model looks like a good teacher mainly on the problems the student could already solve.

Maybe those states are simply lost causes, and nobody could recover from them? They are not. In ALFWorld we handed the same states to a scripted expert that knows the solution, and it finished the task from 88% of the ones where the teacher had failed. The game was still winnable when the teacher sat down.

What is going on is simpler. Some tasks are hard, and they are hard for both models. Playing from the very beginning with no student involved, the teacher fails 53% of the tasks that lie behind uncovered states, and only 7% of the tasks behind covered ones.

The student gets lost on hard tasks, and hard tasks are where the teacher is least reliable.

So the raw teacher data from uncovered states is mostly failed attempts, and a student trained on it is mostly imitating failure. Training on takeovers from uncovered states gives no gain over using all states: 50.1 against 50.2 in ALFWorld, 24.1 against 24.0 in WebShop. That is where the myth comes from. Uncovered states do look like bad places to teach, as long as you train on whatever the teacher happened to do there.

Don't avoid them. Filter them.

If the trouble is the teacher's failures, the fix is to stop learning from them. Every takeover ends with a verdict from the environment: the task was solved or it was not. We keep only the takeovers that solved it.

With that one change, uncovered states go from no better than any other data to the best teaching data we have: 64.5 on ALFWorld and 45.3 on WebShop, from about one in eight takeovers.

The student ends up learning from far less data, and doing far better. Seven-eighths of what the teacher produced at those states was holding it back.

Only real success counts

Running every takeover to its end and checking the outcome is the expensive way to filter, so we tried two cheaper ones. The first keeps just the opening five turns of each takeover, on the theory that a strong model's first moves are sound even when the episode later goes wrong. The second throws out takeovers where the teacher produced a malformed action. Both fall well short, by 8 to 24 points.

A failed takeover does not look like a failure. It is well-formed, it starts out plausibly, and it reads like competent play. The one thing that separates it from a good takeover is whether the task got done.

The weaker the teacher, the more it matters

If the filter works by removing the teacher's failures, it should matter most when the teacher fails most. We swapped teachers in ALFWorld to check. A smaller 14B teacher, which succeeds less often than the 32B one, gains almost three times as much from filtering. A 32B teacher that is handed the expert's plan, and so succeeds on most takeovers, barely needs the filter at all.

This may also explain an apparent disagreement in the literature. A Few Teacher Steps finds that in ALFWorld, short teacher continuations with no success check match or beat success-filtered ones. In our experiments, keeping the first five teacher turns falls well short of filtering. The two setups differ in exactly the place where we find filtering matters: their teacher is a Qwen3-8B trained with RL on the environment, and they sample takeover points uniformly along the trajectory. When we give our own teacher the expert's plan, five unchecked turns match full verification for us too. Both results can hold, each for its own teacher.

There is a hopeful reading of the same result. After filtering, a student taught by the 14B teacher is about as good as one taught by the 32B teacher. A weaker teacher succeeds less often, but when it does succeed, the lesson is worth about as much.

What the filtered student can do

The student trained on verified takeovers at uncovered states beats the usual ways of learning from a teacher. In ALFWorld it is ahead of behavior cloning on the teacher's own demonstrations, and far ahead of vanilla on-policy distillation. In WebShop it is about 8 points ahead of behavior cloning at equal data size when examples are scarce, an edge that fades as data grows.

Two results surprised us.

The student passes its teacher. On ALFWorld tasks neither model has seen, the 1.7B student scores 64.0. The 32B teacher, playing alone with its reasoning mode switched on, scores 53.0. A model nineteen times smaller ends up ahead of the model it learned from, because it only ever studied the teacher's good days.

RL goes much further from here. Reinforcement learning alone reaches 61.4 in ALFWorld. Started from the filtered student, the same RL recipe reaches 91.2, for less compute. Some of that comes from having a teacher at all: RL started from plain behavior cloning also does well, at 81.5 in the one run we have, with the same RL recipe and step budget. The filtered start is still about ten points ahead. Our reading is that a student already shown the way out of its worst states gives RL something to build on, where RL from scratch has to stumble onto those solutions unaided.

What it costs, and where this goes next

One cost should be stated plainly. Fine-tuning a small model on agent data damages what it could do before. Averaged over four general benchmarks (GSM8K, MATH-500, IFEval and MMLU-Redux), the base student scores 75.4, and the student trained on verified takeovers scores 21.3. Mixing the model's own earlier answers back into training as replay data recovers 64.0 without giving up any ALFWorld success. Training with LoRA plus replay recovers 72.8, close to the original, but gives up about 4.5 points on the task. We do not yet have a version that keeps both.

This is ongoing work, and the picture so far comes from two environments, one student, and one family of models. In progress now: more training runs behind every number, including the behavior-cloning-then-RL baseline; a study of how the advantage in WebShop changes as the amount of data grows; a full accounting of what each method costs in compute; and a test of whether the filter still matters once RL follows.

The idea we would keep, whatever the later numbers say, is the change of question. When a student cannot be taught somewhere, it is tempting to ask what is wrong with the student, or with the state. Ask first whether the teacher could do it.
