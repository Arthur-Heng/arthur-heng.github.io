Not All Teacher Help Is Help

A big model's help fails most often exactly where a small agent needs it. Learning only from the help that worked is worth 10 to 21 points, and it changes what reinforcement learning can do afterwards.

Heng Wang · November 12, 2026 · work in progress

Imagine a tutor who is brilliant on the problems you could already solve and wrong on most of the ones you are stuck on. You would still learn from that tutor, but not by copying everything they write down.

That is roughly the position of a small language-model agent learning from a big one. We let a 1.7B student play, had a 32B teacher take over from the places the student reached, and checked each takeover against the environment. Three out of four takeovers failed the task. Where the student was actually stuck, more than five out of six failed.

The recipe everyone uses

What should a small agent take from a big teacher? The common recipe lets the student play and has the teacher take over from states the student reached. DAgger, OEC, OLIVE and many on-policy distillation methods work this way. The appeal is clear: the teacher's guidance lands on the student's own mistakes instead of on states the student will never visit.

The recipe quietly assumes the teacher's help is good help. Published work disagrees on whether that needs checking:

  • OEC and OVCSD keep only takeovers that the environment confirms as successful.
  • A Few Teacher Steps finds that a few unchecked teacher steps work just as well.

Both can't be the general rule. So we measured it: how much of the teacher's help is worth learning, where the useful part sits, and when sorting it matters.

One batch of help, used four ways

A Qwen3-1.7B student learns from a Qwen3-32B teacher in ALFWorld (household tasks, 274 test tasks) and WebShop (shopping, 500 test instructions). Collection is simple:

  1. The student plays training tasks, and we sample one state per task.
  2. We probe each state with 16 student continuations. If none succeeds, the student is stuck there.
  3. The teacher takes over once from each state.
  4. A fresh student is fine-tuned on the teacher's turns.

The same takeovers are then sliced four ways. Because every slice comes from one collection, the cost of gathering the help is the same in every comparison.

Finding 1Only the teacher's successes are worth learning

About three-quarters of the teacher's takeovers fail the task: 77% in both environments. A student trained on the successful quarter beats a student trained on everything, on roughly a quarter of the data.

The rows of the table matter much less than the columns. Restricting takeovers to stuck states adds 4.4 points in ALFWorld and 1.5 in WebShop, and only when the outcome check is already on. Without it, C3 and C1 are indistinguishable.

Can something cheaper than running every takeover to its end do the same job? We tried two shortcuts, and neither closes the gap:

  • Truncation. Keeping the first five teacher turns of every takeover, unchecked, stays 8.5 to 23.6 points below verification.
  • Format filtering. Dropping only takeovers with malformed actions stays 13.1 to 21.4 points below (ALFWorld).

The bad help is not obviously bad. It is well-formed, confident, and starts out plausibly. You find out it was wrong when the task doesn't get done.

Finding 2The teacher is weakest where the student is stuck

If the failures were spread evenly, this would be a story about noise. They are not. The teacher succeeds where the student could have managed alone, and mostly fails where the student is stuck.

Student need and teacher reliability pull in opposite directions.

Two checks explain why:

  • These are not dead ends. A scripted expert solves 88% of the stuck ALFWorld states where the teacher failed. The task was still winnable when the teacher took over.
  • These are tasks the teacher also finds hard. Playing from the very start, the teacher fails 53% of the tasks behind stuck states, against 7% of the tasks behind other states.

So the student and the teacher share a notion of difficulty. The student gets stuck on hard tasks, and hard tasks are where the teacher is least reliable. Guided-OPD reports a related effect for teacher scoring under distribution shift. Here the cause is task difficulty that both models share, and it is measured on what the teacher actually does, not on how it scores.

This is why targeting help at stuck states is not enough by itself. Asking for help "where it is needed" selects exactly the states where the help is worst. Targeting pays only after verification has removed the failures.

Finding 3The weaker the teacher, the more checking pays

We changed the teacher and repeated the comparison in ALFWorld. Within each takeover protocol, a less reliable teacher makes verification worth more:

  • Teacher plays 10 turns, then hands back. A 14B teacher (16% takeover success) gains 28.7 points from verification. The 32B teacher (22%) gains 9.9.
  • Teacher plays to the end. The 32B teacher (28%) gains 18.1. The same teacher given the expert's plan (64%) gains 3.9.

This may be what reconciles the two camps. With the reliable teacher, five unchecked teacher turns do as well as verified takeovers. With the plain teacher they do not (Finding 1). A Few Teacher Steps used a teacher trained with RL on the environment itself, which is the regime where checking adds little. Both camps can be right about their own teacher.

Verification also makes the choice of teacher matter less. A student trained on verified 14B takeovers is statistically level with one trained on verified 32B takeovers (−2.7 points [−7.7, +2.4]). A weaker teacher succeeds less often, but its successes teach about as well.

What it buys

In ALFWorld, verified takeovers beat the standard ways of using a teacher, and they give RL a much better start. RL here is GiGPO with its official recipe, run to a common step budget.

  • The student passes its teacher. On unseen tasks C4 reaches 64.0, against 53.0 for the 32B teacher playing alone with thinking on. A model nineteen times smaller ends up ahead of the model it learned from, because it learned only from the teacher's good days.
  • RL ends about 30 points higher. C4 followed by RL beats RL alone by 29.8 points [25.2, 34.7], at lower compute.
  • The data matters, not just the warm start. Starting RL from C4 beats starting it from behavior cloning by 10.3 points [6.6, 14.1] (one seed, preliminary).

WebShop is the contrast case

WebShop episodes are short, and RL finds successes without help. There the picture is different:

  • Against behavior cloning. Verified takeovers are worth about the same per example as teacher demonstrations. Takeovers at stuck states are more data-efficient when data is small: 52.3 against 44.7 at 259 examples. The gap closes by about 2,500 examples.
  • Against RL. C4 followed by RL ties RL alone at about 70 but gets there sooner: 61.3 against 11.2 at a quarter of RL's compute (equal-compute numbers are from one seed).

The pattern across the two environments: teacher help raises the ceiling where RL explores poorly, and buys speed where RL can explore without it.

Limits and what is next

This is a draft with numbers as of November 12. The recipe is not new: it belongs to the DAgger and OEC family. The contribution is the controlled comparison and the account of when teacher help is worth learning.

  • Scope. Two environments, one student size, one model family. A third environment, TextCraft, was limited by action-format errors and supports no conclusion.
  • Baselines. On-policy distillation is represented by a vanilla implementation only. Stronger variants such as TCOD and PivotOPD are not reproduced yet.
  • Cost. Probing for stuck states does not save compute, and fine-tuning on agent data costs general ability. The mean over four general benchmarks falls from 75.4 to 21.3 (one seed). Mixing in replay data restores it to 64.0 with no loss in ALFWorld.
  • Still running. RL started from unfiltered takeovers will show whether verification still matters once RL follows. A second seed is running for behavior cloning followed by RL, and the last seeds for WebShop RL.

If you take one thing from this: before asking how to get more help from a bigger model, check how much of the help you already have was right. In our setting the answer was a quarter, and the wrong three-quarters sat exactly where the student was looking for guidance.
